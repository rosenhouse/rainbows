Draft board · for review, not the build

How a rainbow forms, from orbit to wavefront

Four drafts to react to before I build anything real: a working physics prototype of the droplet stage, the five scales the zoom will pass through, three visual directions, and three ways the user might drive the zoom.

Draft 1 · live prototype

The droplet, traced for real

This is the physics core the whole piece hangs on, so I built it first. Sunlight enters from the left. Each ray refracts in, reflects off the back of the drop, and refracts out. The refractive index of water depends on wavelength, so the exit angle depends on colour. Drag the wavelength and watch the exit angle move. The bold ray is the one at the caustic, the “rainbow ray”, where the exit angle peaks.

n (water)1.3330
Bow angle42.0°

Index from a Cauchy fit for water at 20 °C. Red exits near 42.4°, violet near 40.6°, which is why red sits on the outside of the primary bow and the order flips in the secondary.

Draft 2 · the zoom path

Five scales, one continuous descent

The zoom needs anchor points where the picture is stable and the caption can land. These are the five I propose, spanning roughly 14 orders of magnitude. Between anchors the camera moves continuously and the previous scale's picture shrinks into a detail of the next.

108 m · orbit

Sun, Earth, shadow

Sunlight arrives as parallel rays. A rainbow is always centred on the antisolar point: the direction of your own shadow.

102 m · landscape

Observer and rain

Sun behind you, rain ahead. The bow is a 42° cone around the shadow line. Low sun means a tall arc.

100 m · rain curtain

Which drops light up

Every drop sends colour in every direction, but only the drops on the 42° cone send it to your eye. The bow is a selection, not a thing.

10-3 m · droplet

Inside one drop

Refract, reflect, refract. The exit angle piles up near 42°, and each colour piles up at its own angle.

10-7 m · wavefront

Why colour bends differently

Light slows in water. Wavefronts crowd together and pivot. Shorter wavelengths slow more, so blue pivots further than red.

Draft 3 · visual direction

Three looks for the same stage

Each phone shows the droplet stage in a different identity. The physics is identical. What changes is the ground, the type, the line quality, and how much the interface talks.

Draft 4 · how the zoom is driven

Three ways to move through the scales

This decision shapes everything else, especially on mobile, so I want to settle it before building the camera.

Scroll-driven

The page scrolls, the camera descends. Captions are pinned in the flow. The most familiar pattern for a mobile reader.

  • Zero learning curve; works one-handed
  • Easy to add prose between stages
  • Harder to “play” at a stage; scrubbing is coarse

Altitude scrubber

One big slider from orbit to wavefront, with soft snapping at the five anchors. Fixed viewport, full-screen canvas, controls in a drawer.

  • Feels like an instrument; precise scrubbing
  • Room for per-stage controls (wavelength, sun angle)
  • Long prose has nowhere to live

Free zoom + rail

Pinch or wheel to zoom anywhere, with a side rail of stage buttons that fly the camera to an anchor.

  • Most exploratory; feels like a map
  • Easy to get lost between anchors
  • Hardest to make smooth on low-end phones
What I need from you

Open questions

Who is it for?

A curious adult, a high-school physics class, a museum kiosk, or a portfolio piece? This sets how much the captions explain and whether readouts like the refractive index are shown or hidden.

Which visual direction, or a blend?

Nightfall, Field Notebook, Instrument. I have a favourite for the zoom itself (A) but the droplet and wavefront stages could borrow B's labelling.

Which driving model?

Scroll-driven, altitude scrubber, or free zoom. My recommendation is the scrubber with scroll as a fallback on very tall content, but scroll-driven is the safer mobile bet.

How deep on the wave picture?

Rays only, wavefronts pivoting at the interface, or a real wave simulation showing phase velocity change? The last is the most beautiful and the most expensive.

Which extras earn a place?

Secondary bow and Alexander's dark band. Supernumerary fringes. Drop-size effects. Sun elevation and the arc's height. Polarisation. Each is a stage or a control; I'd cut most for v1.

Tech constraints?

Plain HTML + Canvas keeps it small and fast on phones. WebGL buys glow and the wave sim. Any framework preference, hosting target, or bundle budget?

Sound, and how much motion?

An ambient bed can sell the descent but many people mute. And should the zoom ever auto-play, or only move on input?
